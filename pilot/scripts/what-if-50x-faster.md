# What If You Were 50x Faster?

Runtime target: ~9 min (≈1,550 words of VO at ~170 wpm)
Narrator: TBD (Asher or Arthur)
Character: Max (same model as the demo)

`[V]` = visual, `[SFX]` = sound, `CARD` = on-screen number card. Everything else is voiceover.

---

## 0:00 Cold open

[V] Max at a bus stop. Bus doors close in his face, bus pulls away.

Max missed the bus. Again.

Not a problem. As of this morning, Max is fifty times faster than you.

[SFX] sharp crack. [V] Bus passengers' hair blows sideways. Max is gone.

He's at work before the bus has left the stop. He's made a coffee. He's answered his emails.

[V] Max at his desk, sipping coffee, smug.

And in about six minutes, he's going to find out that being this fast will cook him alive. Not a figure of speech. We did the maths.

[V] Title card: WHAT IF YOU WERE 50x FASTER?

---

## 0:30 The rules

[V] Max on a white void, rule cards popping in next to him.

Quick ground rules.

Max's body gets the upgrade. Muscles, bones, tendons, reflexes. Everything moves fifty times faster, and it's tough enough not to rip itself apart doing it.

Everything else stays normal. His brain. His stomach. His skin.

And the air around him.

Remember the brain and the air. Both of them are coming back for him.

---

## 0:55 How fast is fifty times?

[V] Max walking next to a speedometer that spins up.

A normal walk is about five kilometres an hour. Max's walk is two hundred and fifty. That's over the speed limit on basically every road on the planet.

CARD: Walk 5 → 250 km/h

A light jog? Five hundred. Faster than any passenger train running today.

CARD: Jog 10 → 500 km/h

And a proper sprint, about twelve hundred and fifty kilometres an hour.

Which is a funny number, because the speed of sound is twelve hundred and thirty-five.

CARD: Sprint 1,250 km/h vs sound 1,235 km/h

Max's sprint is supersonic. Only just. By a hair.

[V] Split screen: Usain Bolt-style stick figure in the blocks, Max in the next lane.

Usain Bolt, the fastest human ever measured, topped out at around forty-four and a half kilometres an hour. Max, out for a stroll, is moving more than five times faster than Bolt at full sprint.

And the hundred metres? In his world record run, Bolt took about a seventh of a second just to react to the gun. In that same seventh of a second, Max is already halfway down the track.

CARD: Max 100 m in 0.29 s. Bolt 9.58 s.

[SFX] crack. [V] Windows rattle along the street.

And every single time he sprints, there's that crack. That's a sonic boom. A small one, but imagine a rifle going off right next to you, every time Max pops to the shops.

Fun fact: in the US, flying faster than sound over land has been banned since 1973. Max isn't a plane. Technically. We're looking into it.

---

## 2:05 The fun part

[V] Fast montage. Each beat gets a stopwatch in the corner.

Okay. Problems later. Let's enjoy this first.

Washing up, normally fifteen minutes. Max does it in eighteen seconds.

The whole flat hoovered in under a minute.

A ten-minute shower takes him twelve seconds. Well, the scrubbing does. The water still falls at normal speed, so mostly Max just stands there, waiting for it to land on him.

[V] Max at a keyboard, hands a blur.

He can type two thousand words a minute. Which sounds amazing, until you remember his brain didn't get the upgrade. So he types way faster than he can think, and every email is just whatever happened to be in his head at the time.

[V] Inbox: "RE: RE: why did you send me a picture of a goose"

Max has had a strange week at work.

---

## 3:00 The high-five

[V] Coworker Dave raises a hand. Slow motion.

And then there are other people.

Dave goes in for a high-five. A normal hand moves at maybe five metres a second. Max's hand moves at two hundred and fifty.

At that speed, a high-five carries about as much energy as a heavy sniper round.

[V] Freeze frame on the moment of contact. Cut away. [SFX] distant car alarm.

This is why the upgrade matters. A normal hand would shatter. Max's hand is fine.

Dave's hand has been better.

So Max learns rule one: around people, he moves like he's carrying a full cup of tea. Always.

---

## 3:35 What actually limits speed

[V] Navy line-art card: sprinter on a treadmill, force arrows under the foot.

Here's something real. In 2010, a team at Southern Methodist University put sprinters on a treadmill that measures every push their feet make. They wanted to know what actually sets the human speed limit.

It isn't how hard your muscles can push. It's how fast. A sprinter's foot is on the ground for about a tenth of a second, and that's all the time you get to shove the planet backwards.

CARD: Ground contact: 0.1 s (you) vs 0.002 s (Max)

Max's foot touches the ground for two thousandths of a second. No muscle on Earth fires that fast. That's exactly the part of him we upgraded.

But the upgrade stops at his skin. And just outside his skin there's air.

---

## 4:15 Problem one: the air

[V] Max mid-sprint, air lines bunching up into a wall in front of him.

At walking pace, you don't notice air. At twelve hundred and fifty kilometres an hour, it stops behaving like air and starts behaving like a wall.

Max is pushing against close to three tonnes of force. Imagine a pickup truck leaning on your chest, and you have to run through it.

The strongest wind gust ever measured on Earth was four hundred and eight kilometres an hour, during Cyclone Olivia in 1996. Max feels about three times that, every time he runs for the bus.

And here's the bit almost everyone gets wrong. Going twice as fast doesn't take twice the power to beat the air. It takes eight times. Power goes up with the cube of your speed.

CARD: 2x faster = 8x power. 50x faster = 125,000x power.

So Max's sprint needs about ten megawatts just to shove the air out of the way. That's roughly what the motors on a high-speed train put out.

One guy. In trainers.

---

## 5:05 Problem two: lunch

[V] Max opens the fridge. It's empty. He looks at the camera.

Ten megawatts has to come from somewhere. For Max, that somewhere is food.

Muscles are only about a quarter efficient. So to put ten megawatts into the air, Max is actually burning forty.

Sprint a hundred metres, which takes him less than a third of a second, and he's burned around two thousand seven hundred calories.

CARD: 100 m sprint = ~2,700 kcal

That's more than most adults eat in an entire day. Gone in a third of a second.

Even walking is brutal. One kilometre at Max's casual stroll costs about twelve hundred calories. His commute is five kilometres. That's around ten Big Macs, and he hasn't sat down yet.

CARD: Commute = ~6,000 kcal ≈ 10 Big Macs

And his stomach didn't get the upgrade. It digests at normal speed. So Max is permanently, desperately hungry, and there's no way to eat fast enough to catch up.

---

## 6:00 Problem three: the heat

[V] Thermometer on screen. Max starts sweating. Then steaming.

Which brings us to the problem that actually kills him.

Remember, muscles are a quarter efficient. The other three quarters of that energy doesn't go anywhere useful. It turns into heat. Inside Max.

One hundred-metre sprint dumps about eight and a half million joules of heat into his body in a third of a second. The human body can get rid of roughly a kilowatt of heat at best, by sweating. That isn't even close.

So Max's core temperature jumps by about thirty-five degrees. From thirty-seven, to seventy-two.

CARD: 37 °C → 72 °C. Egg white sets at ~63 °C.

For reference, an egg white sets at around sixty-three.

One sprint, and Max is soft-boiled.

Even walking heats him up by about a degree every second. Four seconds of strolling and he's in a dangerous fever.

So Max learns rule two. The most important rule of being fifty times faster: mostly, don't be.

---

## 6:55 Problem four: brakes

[V] Max sprinting down a long straight road, a STOP sign rushing at him.

Say Max ignores all that and sprints anyway. Now he has to stop.

His legs can stop instantly. His shoes can't. Stopping comes down to grip, the friction between rubber and road, and that's the same for Max as it is for everyone else.

From a full sprint, Max needs about seven and a half kilometres to stop. That's forty-four seconds of skidding.

CARD: Stopping distance 7.7 km

All that speed has to go somewhere. Stopping from a sprint releases about four million joules, roughly the energy in a kilogram of TNT, and almost all of it goes into the soles of his shoes.

Max doesn't wear out trainers. He sets them on fire.

[V] Overhead map. A giant circle drawn over a whole city.

Turning is even worse. To turn at full speed without sliding out, Max needs a turning circle about thirty kilometres across. He can't turn onto his own street. He can turn around a city.

---

## 7:40 Problem five: the brain

[V] Max's head, normal-speed gears turning slowly inside.

And then there's the thing we said would come back.

His brain. Normal speed.

It takes a person about a quarter of a second to react to something they see. For you, that's nothing. At a walk, you move about one foot-length in that time.

Max, walking, covers seventeen metres before his brain even notices what's in front of him. Sprinting, eighty-seven. Most of a football pitch, completely blind.

CARD: Reaction gap: 17 m walking, 87 m sprinting

Max isn't a superhero. He's a missile with anxiety.

---

## 8:15 The rescue

[V] A quiet street. A kid chases a ball into the road. A car comes round the corner at 50 km/h. The driver is looking at their phone.

Which is why, when it finally matters, Max does the least superhero thing imaginable.

The car is doing fifty. Max spends the first quarter of a second being useless while his brain catches up. In that time, the car moves three and a half metres.

Then Max moves.

And if he grabs that kid at full speed, it isn't a rescue. Going from standing still to Max-speed in a few milliseconds would hit her with thousands of g. So he doesn't sprint. He walks. He picks her up like she's made of glass and carries her two metres to the pavement.

[V] Real-time: the car rolls through the empty spot. The ball bounces off its bumper.

Total time, a bit under half a second. The slowest thing Max has done all week.

The driver never even saw him.

---

## 8:55 The verdict

[V] Max on a park bench, eating a family-size sandwich.

So, is being fifty times faster worth it?

In short bursts, absolutely. Chores, catching dropped phones, emails, badly.

But for actually getting anywhere, the air, the food and the heat all point to the same answer. If Max wants to travel any real distance without cooking himself, the fastest he can keep up is about fifteen to twenty kilometres an hour.

The speed of a decent jogger.

Fifty times faster, and he's stuck at your pace. Because speed was never the hard part. Getting rid of the heat is.

[V] Night. Max's doormat. An official-looking letter slides through the door. The seal is generic, not a real agency.

Max has one more problem.

Twenty-three people reported sonic booms over his street this week.

And somebody would like a word.
