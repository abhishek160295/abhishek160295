# What If You Could See 1 Hour Into the Future?

Thumbnail: Max dangling from a duck-shaped hot-air balloon over the city, Sir Biscuit in his arms, a glowing clock behind. Text: **TOO LATE.** (backup: **DON'T GO.**)
Length: ~6:15 (narration ~5:30 plus holds and set pieces). Narrator only.
**Spine:** at 9 a.m. Max blinks and sees 10 a.m.: he is screaming in a hot-air balloon over the city, holding the dog. He has 60 minutes to stop it. Every move he makes to avoid it drags him closer. A live countdown runs the whole episode.
Running gags: (1) **The narrator begs Max for spoilers.** (2) **Dave is terrified of ducks** (the balloon is a duck). (3) **Sir Biscuit only cares about FOOD** (a sausage starts the chaos). (4) **The pigeon at 10:01.**

Key: `VO` = narration · `▶` = visual · `SLO` = slow motion · `FRZ` = freeze frame · `GFX` = graphic

## 3. Your brain already sees the future (1:15–2:05)

VO: [curious] Here's the thing. You can already see the future. Just not very far.
▶ Max points at the camera. The viewer's silhouette appears.

VO: Light from the world takes time to get through your eyes and into your brain. By the time you see something, it's already about a tenth of a second old.
▶ GFX: a ball flying. A ghost ball trails behind it labelled 0.1 s. Your view lags behind reality.

VO: So your brain cheats. It guesses where things will be and shows you the guess.
▶ GFX: the brain draws a dotted line ahead of the ball. The real ball lands on the line.

VO: It's why a tennis player can return a serve that reaches them in less than half a second. They're not reacting. They're predicting.
▶ A tennis player swings before the ball arrives. A clock: 0.4 s.

VO: [excited] So you've been seeing the future your whole life. About a tenth of a second of it. Max just got an upgrade. Thirty-six thousand times bigger.
▶ GFX: a tiny bar labelled YOU, then a bar that shoots off the screen labelled MAX. Counter: 36,000×.

## Fact check (for the pinned comment)

- Perception lag: the brain takes roughly 0.1 s to process what we see, and it predicts the position of moving objects to make up for it (one explanation of the flash-lag effect, Nijhawan 1994).
- A fast tennis serve reaches the receiver in about 0.4–0.5 s, so returns depend on prediction from the server's body and the ball's early flight.
- 1 hour = 3,600 s; 3,600 ÷ 0.1 s = 36,000×.
- Butterfly effect: Edward Lorenz, 1961, re-ran a weather model with 0.506 instead of 0.506127 and got a completely different result; he named it in a 1972 talk.
- Forecast skill: a 5-day forecast is right about 90% of the time; a 10-day forecast only about half the time (NOAA SciJinks).
- Uncertainty and stress: de Berker et al., Nature Communications, 2016. People were most stressed when the chance of a shock was about 50%, more than when it was certain.
- Self-fulfilling prophecy: the Oedipus story is the classic example; the term was coined by sociologist Robert K. Merton in 1948.
